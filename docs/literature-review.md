# PharmaStock — Literature Review

Task: PHA2-9 · Last updated: 2026-10-16 · Status: draft v0.2 (critical review sections 1–5 added; synthesis and conclusion to be written) · Author: Evelina Penkova · Reviewers: Birkhanym Kazhymukhamet, Abzal Bakhytov

## Purpose and scope

This review supports the diploma project "Development of smart supply chain analytics system for forecasting pharmaceutical inventory and preventing medicine shortages in healthcare organizations" (PharmaStock). Its purpose is to establish what is known about the three problems the system has to solve, to identify the limitations of existing work, and to turn the findings into concrete design decisions for the forecasting module and the shortage-risk logic described in `docs/requirements.md` and `forecasting/README.md`.

The review covers five topic areas:

| Area | Question the review answers |
|---|---|
| Intermittent demand forecasting | Which methods are established for series with many zero periods, how well do they perform, and what do recent machine-learning and probabilistic approaches add? |
| Forecasting medicine demand in healthcare organizations | What has been done on medicine consumption forecasting, with which data, metrics and reported accuracy, including studies from Kazakhstan and the CIS? |
| Drug shortages: causes, impact and prediction | Why do shortages occur, what do they cost, how is Kazakhstan's supply system organised, and can shortages be predicted? |
| Hospital pharmacy inventory management | Which inventory policies and information systems are used in hospital pharmacies and what evidence supports them? |
| Forecasting methodology and evaluation | Which accuracy measures, validation schemes, benchmarks and model families are appropriate for weekly, intermittent, censored demand? |

Out of scope are clinical decision support, pricing and reimbursement policy, and manufacturing-side supply-chain optimisation; they are touched only where a source links them to hospital-level stock-outs.

## Search strategy and inclusion criteria

The review is a structured scoping review, not a systematic review with PRISMA counts. Sources were identified in October 2026 through the Crossref REST API, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, PubMed/PMC, DOAJ and IDEAS/RePEc, through publisher sites (Elsevier, Springer Nature, PLOS, Frontiers, Informa, OECD, FDA), through the Kazakhstan legal databases (adilet.zan.kz and its mirror zakon.uchet.kz) and the website of the Bulletin of the Academy of Civil Aviation. Four foundational papers that the keyword search had missed (demand-pattern categorisation, error measures for intermittent demand, censored demand, evaluation of count-data forecasts) were added by targeted title search in Crossref.

## 1 Intermittent demand forecasting

The intermittent-demand literature starts from Croston's (1972) observation that applying exponential smoothing directly to a series with many zero periods inflates the demand estimate after every demand occurrence; in his worst illustrated case the resulting stock was double what was needed. His remedy, smoothing the non-zero demand size and the inter-demand interval separately and dividing one by the other, is still the reference structure in inventory software and in the project's baselines. The later literature has corrected this estimator rather than replaced it. Syntetos and Boylan (2005) showed on 3,000 automotive series that Croston's estimator is positively biased and that an approximate correction (SBA, factor 1 − α/2) gave the best out-of-sample accuracy among moving average, SES, Croston and SBA. Teunter, Syntetos and Babai (2011) identified a second flaw that SBA leaves untouched: neither estimator updates in zero-demand periods, so a medicine that stops being used keeps its old forecast indefinitely. Their TSB estimator smooths the probability of demand every period and is unbiased at every point in time; in simulation it outperforms Croston and SBA, most clearly when demand becomes obsolete.

The evidence behind these three estimators is uneven, and the difference matters for the project. SBA's advantage was established on real data; TSB's rests on simulation, and its advantage over SBA is smaller when demand is stationary. None of the three produces a predictive distribution, which a shortage-risk flag needs (Türkmen et al., 2021). The categorisation scheme of Syntetos, Boylan and Croston (2005) shares the same point-forecast orientation: demand classes are defined by the regions in which EWMA, Croston or SBA has the lower theoretical mean square error, expressed through the average inter-demand interval and the squared coefficient of variation of demand sizes. The scheme is cheap and interpretable, which is why the project uses it, but it classifies items by which smoothing estimator is better, not by which inventory outcome follows. Pinçe, Turrini and Meissner (2021) make this criticism explicit in their review of the spare-parts literature: across the parametric, bootstrapping and machine-learning streams, method rankings depend on the demand characteristics and on whether evaluation uses forecast errors or inventory performance, and the two can disagree. Wallström and Segerstedt (2010) had shown the mechanism earlier: Croston, SES and two Croston variants rank differently under MAD, MSE and their inventory-oriented measures (periods in stock, number of shortages), and no single measure captures every dimension of error.

Recent machine-learning work has surrounded the classical methods rather than overturned them. Türkmen et al. (2021) reinterpret Croston-type models as discrete-time renewal processes and replace the smoothing with a recurrent network; across five intermittent benchmarks, including the 30,490 M5 series of which 23,401 are intermittent, the neural models improve the P50 loss by about 10% and the P90 loss by over 5% against a static baseline, while the renewal structure alone adds only 2–5%. The same paper reports that SBA and TSB remain competitive on point accuracy and that no model categorically outperforms the classical baselines. Spiliotis et al. (2022) reach a more favourable verdict for machine learning on daily retail SKU demand, where some methods are better on both accuracy and bias, but the gain depends on training across many SKUs (cross-learning), and only the abstract of that study was open for verification. Read together, the two studies locate the gains where PharmaStock can expect them: in probabilistic output and in pooling information across items, not in a large reduction of point error for any single medicine. Two caveats carry over. All of this evidence comes from spare parts and retail, where a zero in the data is normally a true zero; hospital pharmacy data contain zeros caused by empty shelves, which the Croston family treats as periods without demand (section 5). And the benchmark datasets hold thousands of series, whereas the project works with tens of synthetic items now and at most some hundreds of real items later, which limits what a deep or global model can learn.

## 2 Forecasting medicine demand in healthcare organizations

Studies that forecast medicine consumption in healthcare organizations are fewer than the retail and spare-parts studies and are methodologically weaker. Three recent papers show the range. Samniang et al. (2026) is the closest to PharmaStock: weekly dispensing of two Thai provincial hospitals (588 medicines, about 1.43 million linked records over 19 months) is forecast with an XGBoost regressor tuned by a Grey Wolf Optimizer, using calendar and weather features, a chronological 80/20 split and MAE, RMSE and R². The tuned XGBoost reaches MAE 0.81 and R² 0.984, with LightGBM close behind (MAE 1.02, R² 0.977) and random forest far behind (MAE 3.76, R² 0.721). Mbonyinshuti et al. (2022) fit a random forest to monthly district-level consumption of 17 essential medicines in Rwanda's logistics information system (73,066 training and 28,680 test observations) and report R² of 0.71 on the test year. In Kazakhstan, Kumarkanova, Khasenova and Vais (2025) model 1,096 weekly observations of a single antihypertensive drug at a medical centre with ARIMA(1,0,1), simple exponential smoothing (MAPE 4.29% at α = 0.3) and a hybrid of the two, which is best on MAE, RMSE and MAPE.

The three studies share limitations that the project must not inherit. None compares against an intermittent-demand baseline: Samniang et al. compare only tuned machine-learning models, Mbonyinshuti et al. compare nothing, and Kumarkanova et al. compare two classical models on a smooth series where intermittency does not arise. None reports errors per item for rarely consumed medicines; Samniang et al. do not report per-item results, so an R² of 0.984 at aggregate level says nothing about the slow-moving items that cause stock-outs. The metrics are inconsistent and partly inappropriate: R² reported as accuracy (Mbonyinshuti et al.), MAPE on a single series (Kumarkanova et al.), RMSE without a stated scale. None uses stock levels, stock-outs or deliveries, so recorded consumption is treated as demand, and none evaluates several forecast origins: Samniang et al. only vary the test share (10%, 20%, 30%) of one chronological hold-out, and the other two use a single split. The reported accuracies are therefore neither comparable with each other nor transferable to PharmaStock; the project cannot borrow a numerical target from the literature and sets its own after the first baseline experiment (`docs/verification-and-evaluation-plan.md`, section 4.8).

Two studies take routes that are instructive by contrast. Pall et al. (2023) do not forecast consumption but classify next-month shortage severity from dispensing data; their blocked time-series cross-validation with 36 resamples is the most careful validation design among the healthcare sources reviewed (section 3). Cappanera et al. (2022) derive ICU antibiotic demand from predicted therapies rather than from the drug series, which produced fewer total and rush orders than a drug-driven Monte Carlo forecast under the same policy settings; the approach needs patient-level clinical data, which PharmaStock excludes by design (NFR-04), so it marks a boundary rather than an option. For Kazakhstan and the CIS the search found only the single-drug study above. No published work was found that forecasts per-item intermittent consumption across a hospital formulary together with stock data, which is the gap this project occupies; the domestic literature on shortages is policy-oriented (Bukatov et al., 2021; section 3) and contains no forecasting experiments.

## 3 Drug shortages: causes, impact and prediction

The shortage literature is dominated by supply-side explanations from high-income countries. The FDA task force (FDA, 2019) traced the shortages of 2013–2017 to three root causes: no incentive to manufacture less profitable drugs, a market that does not reward mature quality systems, and logistical and regulatory obstacles that slow recovery. Its 2020 recalculation found that after a shortage only 30% of drugs had supply fully restored and 42% saw a significant production increase. The OECD (2024) confirms the picture across its members: shortages were common and rising before COVID-19, concentrate in older off-patent central-nervous-system, cardiovascular and anti-infective medicines, and are attributed to manufacturing and quality problems in 50–60% of cases. Shukar et al. (2021) widen the taxonomy to demand-side causes (just-in-time inventories, seasonal or outbreak-driven spikes) and regulatory causes, and note that there is still no unified definition of a shortage, which complicates monitoring and the comparison of studies. The three sources agree on the implication for a hospital: the dominant causes are upstream and recovery is slow, while the demand-side causes added by Shukar et al. (just-in-time inventories, demand spikes) are the ones a hospital can see in its own data; in both cases the hospital's lever is anticipation. The OECD report accordingly recommends real-time monitoring of supply, demand and stocks, and Shukar et al. list early-warning systems among the operational mitigations.

Kazakhstan's context differs from the OECD setting in a way the international reports do not cover. Procurement for the guaranteed volume of free medical care runs through a single distributor, SK-Pharmacy (Bukatov et al., 2021), and the planning method is prescribed by Order ҚР ДСМ-89 of the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Kazakhstan (MoH RK, 2021): medical organizations form their need for a three-year horizon from last year's actual consumption, register-based patient forecasts and defined daily doses; the inpatient need is last year's consumption minus the forecast year-end balance, where the balance is opening stock plus expected receipts minus expected consumption; organizations must hold between one and six months of stock; and need is consolidated regionally on a fixed calendar in March and April. Statistical forecasting does not appear in the method, and the stock norm is wide enough that the actual buffer is a local decision. The regulation therefore both constrains PharmaStock, whose weekly forecasts must be consistent with the annual cycle and the one-to-six-month norm, and motivates it, because the prescribed inputs (last-year consumption, stock, expected receipts) are exactly what the system imports. The OECD report does not cover Central Asian single-distributor systems, and the search found no quantitative study of shortage dynamics in Kazakhstan, only policy analyses such as Bukatov et al. (2021); the partner data requested by the project will be the first such evidence available to the team.

Prediction of shortages with machine learning is recent. Pall et al. (2023) built the most relevant model: an XGBoost classifier that predicts next-month shortage severity for the 100 most dispensed interchangeable groups in Canada from the dispensing data of 22 pharmacies and 12,269 national shortage records. It reaches 68.8% accuracy (kappa 0.44) and anticipates 10 of 17 impactful shortages, with days of supply, prior shortages and product hierarchy as the main predictors. The same results show the difficulty: precision for the high-severity class is 0.167 and recall 0.333, the holdout is four months, and one author owns the data provider. The study is a precedent for PharmaStock in its features (days of supply, prior shortage episodes) and in its validation design (blocked time-series cross-validation), but its target is a national market shortage, whereas PharmaStock's target is a local stock-out whose causes include delivery delays and demand spikes that no national dataset records. The literature does not separate the two; the project keeps them apart by defining risk from forecast consumption, stock on hand, planned receipts and lead time (FR-08) and by validating alerts against the organization's own stock-outs (FQ-15).

## 4 Hospital pharmacy inventory management

Hospital inventory research answers a different question from forecasting research: given a forecast, which ordering policy minimises stock-outs and cost? Cappanera et al. (2022) give the most complete recent treatment for intermittently demanded critical drugs. In an Italian ICU, a hybrid policy that combines therapy-driven forecasts, a mixed-integer lot-sizing model for reorder points and lot sizes, and reactive rush orders generated at most 15–16 rush orders per year in simulation, against up to 148 for the weekly (S, s) policy that mimicked current practice; the estimated saving is about 38,000 EUR per year for about 10,000 EUR of extra holding cost. Two features of that setting do not transfer. Regular orders arrive in one day and rush orders in two hours, so the cost of a forecast error is a rush order; under the annual central purchase and long delivery lead times described in the project proposal, the same error becomes a stock-out, and the hospital's recourse is redistribution between organizations, which Order ҚР ДСМ-89 permits (MoH RK, 2021). The study also uses patient-level clinical data, which PharmaStock excludes (NFR-04). What transfers is the evaluation logic: the policies are judged by stock-outs and rush orders, not by forecast error, which is the principle behind the project's alert precision and recall.

Evidence on information systems is thin and descriptive. Shen et al. (2024) report a before-and-after case of a supply-processing-distribution system with vendor-managed inventory in an 894-bed specialist hospital: hospital-held inventory fell to about 20% of its previous level, purchasing-plan accuracy rose from 88.1% to 98.6% and purchase frequency fell from 39.9 to 20.1 orders per month. The design has no control group and the comparison periods are reported inconsistently, so the size of the effect is uncertain; but the authors' statement that automated demand prediction has not been achieved and that purchase lists are still reviewed manually names the gap PharmaStock addresses. The international reports reach the same point from the policy side: the OECD (2024) calls for visibility of supply, demand and stocks across the chain, and Shukar et al. (2021) place early-warning systems among operational mitigations. The spare-parts literature adds the methodological warning that forecast accuracy and inventory performance are not the same thing (Pinçe et al., 2021; Wallström & Segerstedt, 2010). PharmaStock's inventory logic is deliberately simple, projected stock against safety stock and lead time (FR-08) with configurable thresholds (FR-11), because the evidence for sophisticated policies comes from settings with fast replenishment; the project's contribution is the forecast and the alert, and the alert is evaluated the way the inventory literature evaluates policies.

## 5 Forecasting methodology and evaluation

**Accuracy measures.** The measurement literature is consistent about what not to do. Hyndman and Koehler (2006) show that percentage errors (MAPE, sMAPE) are undefined or degenerate when actual values are zero or near zero, the normal case for a pharmacy item, and propose MASE as a scale-free measure that is defined for zero-valued series. Wallström and Segerstedt (2010) show on intermittent data that MAD, MSE and inventory-oriented measures rank the same methods differently and recommend complementary measures. Kolassa (2016) goes further: for low-count data, MAD, MASE and weighted MAPE are unsuitable as the only criteria, and the entire discrete predictive distribution should be evaluated with proper scoring rules. Hyndman and Athanasopoulos (2021) add that minimising MAE yields the median forecast, which is zero for a series that is zero in most weeks. Türkmen et al. (2021) observed the underlying pathology empirically: on their benchmarks MASE and MAAPE often rewarded a forecast of zero, which minimises absolute error on a mostly-zero series while being useless for stock control. This is a direct challenge to the project's chosen measures, MAE and WAPE (FR-16), which were selected because pharmacy staff can read them. The review suggests how to keep them honest rather than replace them: compute WAPE only where total actual consumption in the window is positive, as the project already does; report bias separately, since under-forecasting is the direction that causes shortages; report errors per demand class so that a zero-forecast advantage on lumpy items stays visible; check the empirical coverage of the forecast interval; and treat alert precision and recall against actual stock-outs as the decisive comparison. Proper scoring rules are recorded as an option for the experiment reports, not for the user interface.

**Validation.** Bergmeir and Benítez (2012) compared six evaluation procedures over four predictors and found that cross-validation gives more robust model selection than a single last-block hold-out while the theoretical objections to it caused no practical harm; they recommend blocked cross-validation. Hyndman and Athanasopoulos (2021) describe the rolling-forecasting-origin form of this procedure, which the project adopts. Among the healthcare studies only Pall et al. (2023) follow this advice (36 blocked resamples); Mbonyinshuti et al. (2022) and Kumarkanova et al. (2025) use a single chronological split, and Samniang et al. (2026) add only robustness checks with 10%, 20% and 30% test shares of the same hold-out. The project's rolling-origin design with at least three origins (FQ-09) is the practical form of the recommendation for 100–150 weeks of history.

**Benchmarks and global models.** The M5 competition (Makridakis et al., 2022) is the closest public analogue to per-item pharmacy demand: 42,840 hierarchical Walmart series, most of them intermittent, with calendar and price variables, scored by a weighted scaled error across twelve aggregation levels against 24 benchmarks. Its lessons for the project are the discipline of many simple benchmarks, scaled rather than percentage errors, and evaluation at more than one level of aggregation; its limits are one retailer, a 28-day horizon and a ranking tied to one metric. Montero-Manso and Hyndman (2021) explain why global models trained across many series work: they are at least as expressive as local models and generalise better as the number of series grows, so complexity can be afforded on large sets. Spiliotis et al. (2022) report the same cross-learning gain for daily SKU demand, although only at abstract level. The caveat is the size condition: with a few hundred items the advantage of a global gradient-boosting model over per-item SBA or TSB may be small, which is why the project treats a negative result as a legitimate finding (risk R-03 in `docs/project-management/risks.md`) and selects a model per medicine group on the validation window.

**Gradient boosting.** Gradient-boosted trees are the learner of choice in the applied sources: Samniang et al. (2026) find XGBoost and LightGBM within about 0.2 MAE of each other and far ahead of random forest, and Pall et al. (2023) select XGBoost over ARIMA and ETS. The common thread is that boosting on lag, rolling and calendar features turns forecasting into a tabular problem that can be trained globally; none of these studies reports whether the trees were adapted to intermittency, for example through the loss function, which the project's experiments will have to establish. LightGBM itself (Ke et al., 2017) was designed and benchmarked on generic tabular tasks, so lag features and a count-appropriate loss are the project's responsibility, not the library's.

**Censored demand.** Sachs and Minner (2014) formalise what the healthcare studies ignore: when a stock-out truncates observed sales, the observation is a lower bound on demand, and estimators that treat it as demand are biased. Their estimator recovers lost sales from the timing of sales within the day; weekly pharmacy exports carry no such timing, so PharmaStock can mark censored weeks (FR-17) but cannot estimate the lost demand from them. Exclusion of censored weeks is therefore the default (FQ-10), imputation by expected demand is an experiment, and learning the zeros, the implicit practice of the reviewed consumption-forecasting studies (Table 1 nos. 10–12) and of spreadsheet planning, is kept only as a control.
